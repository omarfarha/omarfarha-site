I've sat across from a lot of founders in the exact moment I'm about to describe — the one where the numbers stop working, the story they've been telling investors starts to sound thin even to them, and they have to decide whether they still believe it. Most people don't talk about that moment. They talk about it after, once it's resolved, once it makes a good story. So I want to talk about someone else's version of it first, because it says more about the decision than the outcome does.

In 2008, Elon Musk was, by any reasonable measure, failing. Tesla was weeks from running out of cash. SpaceX had just watched its rocket blow up for the third time in a row — not a delay, not a setback, an actual explosion, three times. He had put everything he had into both companies, was borrowing money to cover rent, and would later call it the worst year of his life. Nobody would have blinked if he'd shut it down. That's usually the sane move.

He didn't take it. He put $40 million more into Tesla that he mostly didn't have, took out loans against what remained of his reputation, and tried the rocket a fourth time. It worked. I don't think that fact — that it eventually worked — should carry as much weight in this story as it usually does. Plenty of people make the same bet and it doesn't work, and we never hear about them, because failure without a happy ending doesn't get written up. Survivorship bias is doing a lot of quiet work in most stories about relentless founders.

What I actually find useful here isn't "he never gave up," which is the version of this story everyone already knows and which is true of almost nobody's real life, mine included. What I find useful is narrower: he seems to have been unusually good at telling the difference between a problem with the plan and a problem with his nerve. Most people I work with lose that distinction exactly when they need it most. A bad quarter starts to feel like proof the whole idea was wrong. A bad year starts to feel like a referendum on them personally. Musk, by his own account, was terrified and broke in 2008 — he wasn't unaffected by it — but he doesn't seem to have confused being scared with being wrong.

I want to be careful not to turn this into hero worship, because I think that's where these stories usually go off the rails, and it's part of why I stopped writing them this way. The obsessiveness that got three rockets rebuilt after three explosions is the same trait, pointed slightly differently, that shows up in a man who's been married three times, twice to the same person, and who by his own kids' accounts is not always present. Singlemindedness isn't a virtue in the abstract. It's a resource, and like any resource, what it costs depends entirely on where you point it and what you're willing to spend to keep it running. I don't think you can extract "relentless focus" from someone's story as a clean lesson without also asking what it took out of the people around them. Usually the story just leaves that part out.

What I keep coming back to, working with people who are mid-collapse on something they've built — an early-stage founder, a gallery about to lose its space, an artist watching a body of work fail to land — isn't "believe in yourself harder." It's the much smaller, less quotable question underneath it: is this actually not working, or does it just feel unbearable right now? Those are different problems. One means change the plan. The other means survive the week. Confusing them is how good ideas die early, and also how bad ideas get another two years they didn't deserve. The skill isn't stubbornness. It's knowing, in a moment that doesn't feel clear at all, which kind of moment you're actually in.

I don't know that Musk would put it that way. But I think that's the part of 2008 worth taking with you — not the myth of the man who never quits, but the much more practical fact that he kept the two questions separate when everything in him had every reason to collapse them into one.
